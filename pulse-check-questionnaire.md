# The Pulse Check — Facilitator Questionnaire
**Status:** Working draft v0.1 · Drafted Aug 10, 2026 · Internal, not client-facing

The instrument for running the free 30-minute review. It is **not** the paid intake — `audit-intake-questionnaire.md` is a 20-minute homework form for the Digital Presence Plan, and sending it here would break the promise on `/consultation/` ("No prep required. Come exactly as you are.").

**What this call owes the client:** three observations and one recommendation, delivered as a one page written readout within 48 hours. Nothing more, and nothing less — the promise is on the page in `deliverIntro`, so it is a commitment, not a nice-to-have.

**What it must not become:** a graded scorecard, a full assessment, or a roadmap. Those are the Plan's deliverables. If you deliver them free, the Plan has nothing left to sell and the client learns the paid work was already free.

---

## How the 30 minutes are shaped

| Minutes | Segment | Your job |
|---|---|---|
| 0–3 | Orientation | Set the frame, get the one-sentence version of what they do |
| 3–9 | **FIND** — can people find you? | How people arrive today, and where they'd look |
| 9–16 | **TRUST** — can they trust you? | What a stranger sees, live, on screen |
| 16–22 | **CHOOSE** — is it easy to choose you? | The path from interested to in touch |
| 22–27 | Constraints | Who does the work, how much time, what's already been tried |
| 27–30 | Close | Name the one thing out loud, say what arrives in 48 hours |

**Run two tracks at once.** Track one is the conversation below. Track two is you, quietly, with their site open — you're looking at the thing while they describe it, and the gap between the two is where most of the observations come from. Never narrate track two mid-answer; note it and come back.

**Talk less than half the time.** If you're explaining more than you're asking before minute 22, you've turned a review into a pitch.

---

## Before the call — 5 minutes, yours not theirs

Three fields collected at booking (name, email, and **website or the name of the organization** if there's no site). That's not prep for them; it's what lets you walk in already having looked.

With those five minutes, in this order:

- ☐ Search their organization name. What owns page one? Note anything confusable.
- ☐ Open the homepage on a **phone-width window**. Start a timer at first paint — you want a real number for how long the fold takes.
- ☐ Read the homepage for five seconds, then look away. Write down what you think they do and who for, before you know the answer. **Keep this note.** It's often observation #1.
- ☐ Find them on the one or two channels their audience would actually use. Is the account alive?
- ☐ Look for a way to contact or book. Count the clicks from homepage to submitted.

Do not run tools, crawl the site, or build a checklist. Five minutes, four tabs.

## 0–3 · Orientation

Say this, roughly:

> This is thirty minutes and it's a conversation, not an evaluation. I'll ask about three things — whether people can find you, whether they trust you when they do, and whether it's easy to choose you. I'll have your site up while we talk. In forty-eight hours you'll get one page from me: three things I noticed and the one I'd do first. It's yours either way.

1. **In a sentence or two — what do you do, and who for?** *(As you'd tell a neighbor.)*
2. **What made you book this call now?** *(The itch. Do not accept "just curious" — follow with: "What made this week the week?")*
3. **If the next six months went well, what's different?** *(More of what, specifically — clients, donations, bookings, calls?)*

**Listen for:** a trigger event (a lost deal, a new competitor, a grant deadline, a bad month). The trigger usually tells you which of the three questions is really the live one, and you should weight the rest of the call toward it.

---

## 3–9 · FIND — can people find you?

4. **How do people find you today, as far as you know?** *(Let them rank it themselves: word of mouth, search, social, referrals, directories, events.)*
5. **Where would you expect someone to look for you if they'd never heard of you?**
6. **Where do you exist online besides the website?** *(Take the list as they say it — what they forget to mention is data.)*
7. **Is there anything out there about you that's wrong right now?** *(Old address, a former staff member, an account nobody can get into.)*

**Track two, while they answer:** does the branded search return them cleanly? Are the channels they just listed actually current, or dormant with a two-year-old post? Do the channels link back to the site — and does the site link out to them?

**Listen for:** "everything is word of mouth" said with pride. That's a healthy business with no second engine, and it's fragile in a way the owner rarely feels until it isn't.

---

## 9–16 · TRUST — can they trust you when they get there?

This is the segment to share screens for if the tool allows. Looking together beats describing.

8. **Say I'm a stranger who just landed on your homepage. What do you want me to feel in the first five seconds?**
   → Then tell them what you actually wrote down in your five-second test. Do this gently and do it out loud — it is the single most useful thing that happens on the call.
9. **Who wrote what's there now, and when was it last meaningfully touched?**
10. **What makes someone pick you over the alternative?** → **Is that anywhere on the site?** *(It usually isn't.)*
11. **What do you have that proves it?** *(Testimonials, results, credentials, photos of real work, named clients — whatever's true for them.)*
12. **When someone's deciding, what are they nervous about?** *(Price, commitment, being sold to, whether you're still in business.)*

**Track two:** is the proof above the fold or buried three scrolls down? Is the same logo, name, and tone on the site as on the channels? Anything visibly broken — images, links, a copyright year that ended two years ago, a form that errors?

**Listen for:** a good answer to Q10 and Q11 that exists only in their head. A trust observation is almost always "you already have this, it's just not where anyone can see it" — which is a far better observation than "you need more."

---

## 16–22 · CHOOSE — is it easy to choose you?

13. **What's the one thing you most want a visitor to do?** *(If the answer is a list, ask which one they'd take if they could only have one.)*
14. **Walk me through what someone does from finding you to being in touch.** *(Let them narrate it. Count the steps yourself.)*
15. **How fast do you actually reply to a new inquiry — honestly?**
16. **How does a person know what happens next, before they contact you?** *(Price ranges, process, what a first meeting is, whether you're taking new clients.)*
17. **Do you know where your last few inquiries came from?**

**Track two:** count the clicks from homepage to submitted. Is there one clear next step, or four competing ones? Does the phone number tap on mobile? Do the off-site channels dead-end, or do they carry the same action?

**Listen for:** a slow honest answer to Q15. Response time is the cheapest fix in the whole practice and it is frequently the most valuable observation you'll make.

---

## 22–27 · Constraints — what's actually possible for them

Ask these or the recommendation will be unusable.

18. **Who would do the work — you, someone on the team, or someone hired?**
19. **Realistically, how much time a week could go to this?** ☐ under 1 hr ☐ 1–3 hrs ☐ 3+ ☐ none, it'd have to be done for us
20. **What have you already tried, and what happened?** *(Prevents you recommending the thing that failed for them in 2024.)*
21. **Is there anything paid running right now?** *(Ads, an SEO service, a social manager, subscriptions nobody remembers.)*
22. **Does anyone else have to say yes to a change like this?**

**Listen for:** a locked asset — a domain, a site login, or a Google profile controlled by a vendor or an ex-employee. If you hear it, it very likely becomes the recommendation regardless of everything else, because nothing else can be done until it's resolved.

---

## 27–30 · Close

- **Name the one thing out loud, before you hang up.** "If I only got to tell you one thing today, it'd be ___." Don't save it for the document. The value they feel in the room is what makes them open the email.
- **Say what arrives:** one page, within 48 hours, three observations and one recommendation, theirs to keep.
- **Answer the DIY question honestly.** If it's something they can do themselves, say so plainly. That's the offer — "An honest call" is card three on the page.
- **Only then, and only if it fits:** "If you want the whole picture rather than the one thing, that's the Digital Presence Plan — happy to send details." One sentence. No deck, no pricing conversation, no follow-up close.
- **Last question, always:** "What did I not ask about that I should have?"

## Conditional modules — use only if triggered

**No website yet.** Skip most of TRUST. Ask instead: Where do people currently reach you? What's the one page that would have to exist for someone to say yes? What's the name — do you own the domain? Is anything already published in your name that you don't control? *(The recommendation here is almost always "one page, one action, this month" — not a site build.)*

**They raise ads or boosting.** Run the four gates from `/consultation/` verbatim, as questions: Is there an offer a stranger immediately gets? Is there a page that turns visitors into action? Do you know your customer well enough to target them? Could you tell whether a dollar spent came back? Any "no" and the honest answer is: reach amplifies what's there, it doesn't fix it.

**Nonprofit.** Which audiences do you need online — donors, volunteers, participants, grantmakers? Does each have a path, or does the site serve only donors? Where does a gift actually happen, and is recurring offered? *(Do not turn this into a grants conversation.)*

**Professional practice.** Are you taking new clients — and is that clear online? How do people book? Which directories carry you, and are they current? *(Watch testimonial compliance rules; don't recommend review-gathering to a clinician without checking.)*

**Sells products.** Where do people buy — your site, a marketplace, in person? Where do you think people drop off? *(Resist diagnosing checkout on a 30-minute call; note it for the Plan.)*

## Choosing the one recommendation

Three observations are easy. The single recommendation is the whole product, so pick it by rule, not by feel:

1. **Blocker first.** A locked domain, an inaccessible profile, wrong information published, a broken contact path — anything that makes other work impossible outranks everything else.
2. **Then the biggest gap between what's true and what's visible.** They have the proof, the credential, the result, the clear answer — and a stranger can't see it. Cheapest distance between where they are and where they want to be.
3. **Then the leak they can actually plug** with the capacity from Q18–19. A recommendation that needs a team they don't have is not a recommendation.
4. **Break ties with the trigger from Q2.** Two candidates of equal weight: pick the one that touches the reason they booked.

**Never recommend a rebuild.** "Rebuild the site" is not an observation, it isn't actionable inside 48 hours, and it reads as the sales move it would be. If the site genuinely needs replacing, the honest one-liner is what the *next decision* is — not the project.

## The 48-hour readout — one page, this shape

> **What I noticed** — three observations, one short paragraph each. Each one names the specific thing you looked at. "Your homepage" is weak; "the headline above your booking button" is an observation.
>
> **What I'd do first** — the single recommendation. What it is, why this one before the others, and roughly what it takes. Say plainly whether it's a do-it-yourself or a bring-someone-in.
>
> **What I didn't cover** — one or two lines. Honest scope: thirty minutes buys the biggest thing, not the whole picture. This is also the only place the Plan is mentioned, and it's mentioned as an option, not a close.

Rules for writing it:

- **No score, no grade, no letter, no percentage.** The scorecard belongs to the Plan.
- **No invented numbers.** No "this is costing you roughly $X a month" — you don't have their conversion data and the estimate would be theater. Describe the mechanism, not a figure.
- **Their words, not yours.** Quote how they described their own goal back at them. It proves you listened and it makes the recommendation feel like theirs.
- **One page means one page.** If it runs to two, you're writing the Plan for free.
- **Send it whether or not they seem like a fit.** The promise was unconditional; a readout withheld from a non-prospect is the tell that it was never a gift.

## Boundaries — what does not happen on this call

- No pricing conversation, no proposal, no deck.
- No grading, scoring, or ranked list of everything wrong. "The single biggest opportunity — not a list of everything" is the page's own phrasing.
- No access requests. No analytics logins, no CMS credentials, no admin invites — that's the Plan's Day 3 checklist.
- No competitor analysis, no keyword research, no technical crawl. If you find yourself opening a tool, you've left the review.
- No second call to "go over the findings." The findings are the one page. A second call is the Plan's discovery, and it's paid.
- **If they push for the full picture in the room:** "That's exactly what the Plan is for — I'd rather do it properly than guess at it in the time we have." Then send the one page anyway.
